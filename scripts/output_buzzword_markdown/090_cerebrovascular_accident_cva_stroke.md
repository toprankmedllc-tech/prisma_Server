## Buzzword Question

A 65-year-old man with long-standing hypertension is found unresponsive at home. On arrival to the emergency department, he is awake and able to communicate only by blinking and vertical eye movements. He cannot move his limbs, face, or tongue, and he cannot speak, but he comprehends commands. The most likely site of infarction is:

## Buzzwords Used

able to communicate only by blinking and vertical eye movements; cannot move his limbs, face, or tongue, and he cannot speak, but he comprehends commands

## Answer Options

A) Bilateral medial medullary regions
B) Ventral pons
C) Lateral medulla
D) Thalamus (bilateral)

## Correct Answer

B) Ventral pons

## Explanation

Locked-in syndrome results from infarction of the ventral pons, most often from basilar artery occlusion. The corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts are destroyed bilaterally (quadriplegia and anarthria), while the reticular activating system (consciousness) and the midbrain centers controlling vertical gaze and blinking are SPARED,  the patient is fully awake but cannot move.

## Buzzword Combination (Correct)

Quadriplegia + anarthria + preserved consciousness and vertical gaze = locked-in syndrome (ventral pons / basilar artery)

## Wrong Options

### Bilateral medial medullary regions

Medial medullary infarcts cause contralateral arm/leg weakness, contralateral loss of vibration/proprioception, and ipsilateral tongue deviation. Even bilateral involvement does not produce the awake/blink-only pattern of locked-in syndrome.

**Buzzword Combo:** Contralateral arm/leg weakness + ipsilateral tongue deviation = medial medullary (ASA)

### Lateral medulla

Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome produces dysphagia, hoarseness, ipsilateral Horner, ataxia, and the crossed sensory pattern , not bilateral quadriplegia or locked-in state.

**Buzzword Combo:** Crossed sensory + Horner + dysphagia = lateral medullary (PICA, Wallenberg)

### Thalamus (bilateral)

Thalamic strokes typically cause contralateral hemisensory loss, sometimes followed by delayed central post-stroke pain (Dejerine-Roussy / thalamic pain syndrome). Bilateral paramedian thalamic infarcts produce altered consciousness and vertical gaze palsy, the opposite of locked-in (where vertical gaze is preserved).

**Buzzword Combo:** Contralateral hemisensory loss + delayed burning pain = thalamic stroke (Dejerine-Roussy)

## Related Concepts

Wallenberg syndrome (PICA); Medial medullary syndrome (ASA); Thalamic pain syndrome (Dejerine-Roussy)
