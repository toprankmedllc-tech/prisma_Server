## Chief Complaint

Insidious 18-month decline in short-term memory and instrumental activities of daily living

## Vignette

A 74-year-old retired schoolteacher is brought to clinic by her daughter, who has noticed gradual changes over the past 18 months. She has had progressive difficulty remembering recent conversations, repeatedly misplaces her keys and glasses, and recently became lost while driving to a familiar grocery store. Her daughter has noticed that she struggles to find common words and has trouble using familiar kitchen tools. She has remained socially appropriate and her sleep is unchanged. She has no resting tremor, no hallucinations, and no episodes of acting out her dreams at night. Her past medical history is significant for hypertension and depression treated with sertraline. On examination, vital signs are normal. She is alert and oriented to person but not to date. Gait is normal and there is no urinary incontinence. Motor strength, sensation, and reflexes are normal throughout, and her plantar responses are downgoing. Cranial nerve and eye examinations are unremarkable. She scores 19/30 on the Mini-Mental State Examination, missing items on recall and orientation. Her medication list, complete blood count, and basic metabolic panel are unremarkable.

## Key Symptoms

Gradual decline in short-term memory; getting lost in familiar places; word-finding difficulty; difficulty using common tools; intact gait, sleep, and continence; no parkinsonian features; no hallucinations

## Labs

CBC and basic metabolic panel normal; further labs not yet obtained

## Physical Exam

Alert; oriented to person only; MMSE 19/30 (lost points on recall, orientation, and language); normal gait; intact strength, sensation, reflexes throughout; downgoing plantars; no parkinsonian signs; no focal cranial nerve findings

## Main Clue

Insidious, progressive amnestic-predominant cognitive decline in an elderly woman with intact gait, continence, and motor exam, clinical pattern most consistent with Alzheimer disease

## Supporting Clue

Multiple potentially reversible mimics remain on the differential and have not been excluded: hypothyroidism, vitamin B12 deficiency, neurosyphilis, normal pressure hydrocephalus, depression-related pseudo-dementia, and medication effect, these must be evaluated BEFORE labeling the patient with Alzheimer disease

## Question

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in evaluation?

## Correct Answer

B) Obtain thyroid-stimulating hormone, vitamin B12, treponemal serology, and MRI of the brain to evaluate for reversible causes of dementia
