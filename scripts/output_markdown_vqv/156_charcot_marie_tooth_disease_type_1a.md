## Chief Complaint

Five years of progressive distal lower-extremity weakness and gait disturbance in a young man with a strong family history of similar findings

## Vignette

A 22-year-old college student presents with a slowly progressive 5-year history of trips and falls. He says his feet hang downward and inward when he walks, and he has had to wear orthotic braces and lift his knees higher than usual to avoid scraping his toes on the floor. His ankles have always been weak and he has had difficulty wearing standard shoes because his feet have very high arches and his toes curl downward at the middle joint and bend back at the tip. On examination, he is alert and well-nourished. His feet have high longitudinal arches and his toes are flexed at the proximal interphalangeal joints with hyperextension at the distal joints. There is marked thinning of his calves and distal lower extremities with relative preservation of thigh bulk, giving his legs the appearance of inverted bottles. He has 4/5 strength in dorsiflexion and toe extension bilaterally and 5/5 strength in proximal lower-extremity muscles. Hands and forearms show mild distal weakness and atrophy of the intrinsic hand muscles. Deep tendon reflexes are absent at the ankles and reduced at the knees. Vibration and proprioception are mildly reduced at the toes. Light touch sensation is preserved. Both parents and a paternal uncle have had similar foot deformities and gait difficulties since young adulthood. Nerve conduction studies show markedly and uniformly slowed conduction velocities in all motor and sensory nerves examined.

## Key Symptoms

Slowly progressive distal symmetric weakness; foot drop with high stepping gait; high-arched feet (pes cavus); curled toes with proximal interphalangeal flexion and distal interphalangeal hyperextension (hammer toes); marked distal calf and lower-extremity atrophy with preserved thigh bulk (stork-leg or inverted-bottle appearance); intrinsic hand muscle atrophy; absent ankle reflexes; mildly reduced distal vibration and proprioception with preserved light touch; autosomal-dominant family history; markedly slowed uniform nerve conduction velocities

## Labs

CBC: WBC 6.4 × 10⁹/L, Hgb 14.6 g/dL, Plt 230 × 10⁹/L; BMP: Na 139 mEq/L, K 4.0 mEq/L, Cr 0.8 mg/dL, glucose 88 mg/dL; HbA1c 5.2%; TSH 1.8 mIU/L (0.4 to 4.0); Vitamin B12: 480 pg/mL (200 to 900); HIV antibody negative; RPR non-reactive; Serum protein electrophoresis without monoclonal spike.

## Imaging Findings

MRI of the lumbar spine unremarkable. Nerve conduction studies: markedly and uniformly slowed conduction velocities (less than 38 m/s) in all motor and sensory nerves examined, with prolonged distal motor latencies. EMG shows chronic neurogenic changes in distal muscles

## Physical Exam

Alert; high-arched feet with hammer-toe deformity; marked distal lower-extremity atrophy with stork-leg appearance; intrinsic hand muscle atrophy; distal greater than proximal weakness; absent ankle reflexes, reduced knee reflexes; mildly reduced distal vibration and proprioception; intact light touch; high-stepping foot-drop gait

## Main Clue

Chronic, slowly progressive, distal symmetric peripheral neuropathy with characteristic foot deformity (pes cavus, hammer toes), distal muscle atrophy (stork-leg appearance), and an autosomal-dominant family history is the classic clinical picture of Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease. The markedly and uniformly slowed nerve conduction velocities indicate a primary DEMYELINATING (rather than axonal) neuropathy, consistent with CMT type 1, of which the most common subtype is CMT1A (PMP22 duplication on chromosome 17)

## Supporting Clue

In CMT1A, sural nerve biopsy is the histologic gold standard and shows ONION-BULB formations: concentric layers of Schwann cell processes wrapped around individual axons, reflecting repeated cycles of demyelination and remyelination. The same finding is seen in other chronic demyelinating neuropathies (e.g., chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy), but in the context of this hereditary distal-predominant clinical picture it is essentially pathognomonic
