## Vignette

A pulmonologist at a regional cancer center collaborates with epidemiologists at a school of public health to design a prospective cohort study examining the relationship between cigarette smoking and the development of lung adenocarcinoma. The study recruits 500 current smokers (≥20 pack-years) and 500 age- and sex-matched lifetime non-smokers from the same geographic region, all without prior cancer diagnoses at enrollment. Participants undergo annual low-dose CT chest scans and pulmonary function testing. They are followed for 10 years with standardized follow-up.  At the conclusion of the 10-year study, 50 participants in the smoker cohort and 10 participants in the non-smoker cohort have received confirmed diagnoses of lung adenocarcinoma based on tissue biopsy. There were 12 deaths from non-pulmonary causes and 8 patients lost to follow-up over the study period, distributed roughly equally between groups. For the purposes of this analysis, the investigators use the full denominators (500 per group) as the denominator for risk calculations.  The investigators present their findings at the American Thoracic Society Annual Meeting and are asked by an audience member to clarify both the relative risk and its clinical interpretation. A statistician in the audience raises a question about whether relative risk or odds ratio would be more appropriate given the study design.  Which of the following best represents the relative risk (RR) of lung adenocarcinoma in smokers compared to non-smokers in this study, and what does an RR of this magnitude indicate?

## Key Symptoms

Smokers: 50/500 develop lung adenocarcinoma (10%); Non-smokers: 10/500 develop lung adenocarcinoma (2%); RR = 10%/2% = 5; cohort study; RR appropriate (not OR); ARR = 8%; NNT = 12.5


## Labs

Risk in smokers = 50/500 = 0.10 (10%); Risk in non-smokers = 10/500 = 0.02 (2%); RR = 0.10/0.02 = 5.0; ARR = 0.10 - 0.02 = 0.08 (8%); RRR = (0.10-0.02)/0.10 = 80%

## Main Clue

RR = Incidence in exposed / Incidence in unexposed = 10%/2% = 5; smokers have 5x the risk; RR is the correct measure for cohort studies (not OR); RR ≠ absolute risk difference

## Supporting Clue

Cohort study measures incidence directly → use RR; case-control studies measure odds → use OR; RR > 1 = increased risk; ARR = 8% (different from RR); NNT applies to interventions not exposures but numerically = 1/ARR = ~13
