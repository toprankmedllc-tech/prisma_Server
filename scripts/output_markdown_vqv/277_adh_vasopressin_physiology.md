## Chief Complaint

Confusion and difficulty arousing after prolonged water intake during training run

## Vignette

A 24-year-old male with no significant past medical history is brought to the emergency department by his roommate after being found confused and difficult to arouse. His roommate reports that the patient has been training for a marathon and consumed approximately 6 liters of water over the past four hours during a long training run. He denies any alcohol or illicit drug use on the patient’s behalf. The patient is not on any medications. He has no history of psychiatric illness, kidney disease, or endocrine disorders.

His vitals are notable for a blood pressure of 122/78 mmHg, heart rate of 68 bpm, temperature of 36.9°C, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air.

On physical exam, he is a confused male who is oriented only to person. He does not follow commands consistently. There is no jugular venous distension. His mucous membranes are moist. No peripheral edema is noted. Skin turgor is normal. Neurological exam reveals diffuse hyperreflexia and mild bilateral upper extremity tremor.

Labs are drawn and reveal the following: serum sodium 118 mEq/L, serum potassium 3.8 mEq/L, serum osmolality 242 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 220 mOsm/kg, urine sodium 18 mEq/L, BUN 8 mg/dL, creatinine 0.7 mg/dL, and serum glucose 92 mg/dL. ADH level is measured and found to be appropriately suppressed. Thyroid function tests and cortisol levels are normal.

Which of the following best describes the primary physiological mechanism responsible for this patient’s hyponatremia?

## Main Clue

ADH is appropriately suppressed and urine is already dilute at 220 mOsm/kg, meaning the kidney is doing everything physiologically correct — the system is simply overwhelmed by the sheer volume of water ingested faster than maximal free water excretion can handle

## Supporting Clue

Urine sodium of 18 mEq/L and low BUN reflect euvolemic to mildly hypervolemic dilutional state; normal hemodynamics confirm no volume depletion stimulus for non-osmotic ADH release; absence of any hormonal or structural pathology confirmed by normal cortisol and thyroid function
