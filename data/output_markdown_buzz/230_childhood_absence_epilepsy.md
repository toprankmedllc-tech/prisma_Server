## Buzzword Question

A 7-year-old has multiple brief daily episodes of staring with rapid eyelid blinking lasting about 10 seconds, with no postictal confusion. Episodes are reproducibly triggered by hyperventilation during the office visit. EEG shows generalized 3 Hz spike-and-wave discharges. The first-line drug for this disorder is:

## Buzzwords Used

Child; brief staring spells with eyelid blinking; no postictal confusion; hyperventilation-triggered; 3 Hz generalized spike-and-wave on EEG

## Answer Options

A) Phenytoin
B) Ethosuximide
C) Carbamazepine
D) Lamotrigine

## Correct Answer

B) Ethosuximide

## Explanation

Childhood absence epilepsy classically shows brief staring with 3 Hz generalized spike-and-wave discharges. Ethosuximide blocks T-type calcium channels in thalamic neurons and is the first-line drug, with valproate as a strong alternative if other seizure types coexist.

## Buzzword Combination (Correct)

Child + brief staring + 3 Hz spike-and-wave + first-line therapy = T-type Ca channel blocker → ethosuximide

## Wrong Options

### Phenytoin

Phenytoin treats focal and generalized TONIC-CLONIC seizures and can WORSEN absence seizures. It is not appropriate here.

**Buzzword Combo:** Tonic-clonic or focal seizures + Na channel blocker = phenytoin

### Carbamazepine

Carbamazepine is first-line for focal seizures and for trigeminal neuralgia. It can paradoxically worsen absence and myoclonic seizures.

**Buzzword Combo:** Focal seizures or trigeminal neuralgia + Na channel blocker = carbamazepine

### Lamotrigine

Lamotrigine is broad-spectrum and effective in many seizure types, but it is not first-line for childhood absence epilepsy.

**Buzzword Combo:** Broad-spectrum AED + Stevens-Johnson risk + slow titration = lamotrigine

## Related Concepts

Valproate (broad-spectrum alternative for absence with other seizure types); Lennox-Gastaut syndrome (slow spike-and-wave, multiple seizure types); Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (morning myoclonic jerks, generalized polyspike-and-wave)
