## Buzzword Question

A 14-year-old boy with slowly progressive proximal muscle weakness has been able to walk independently since age 5. His creatine kinase is 8,200 U/L (markedly elevated). Muscle biopsy shows reduced but present dystrophin. Genetic testing reveals a large in-frame deletion in the DMD gene. Which of the following best explains why his phenotype is milder than classic Duchenne muscular dystrophy?


## Buzzwords Used

14-year-old; walking independently; elevated CK; reduced but present dystrophin; in-frame deletion; DMD gene; milder phenotype

## Answer Options

A) An in-frame deletion preserves the reading frame, producing a truncated but partially functional dystrophin protein (Becker MD); a frameshift deletion in DMD produces a premature stop codon, no functional dystrophin, and a severe phenotype 
B) The in-frame deletion causes complete absence of dystrophin, which paradoxically results in a milder phenotype due to compensatory utrophin upregulation 
C) Becker MD results from a point mutation in the dystrophin gene, while Duchenne results from a deletion
 D) His milder phenotype is explained by X-inactivation protecting one copy of the dystrophin gene

## Correct Answer

A

## Explanation

The '21-amino acid rule' (Monaco): DMD gene mutations causing out-of-frame (frameshift) deletions disrupt the reading frame, producing a premature stop codon → complete absence of dystrophin → severe Duchenne phenotype (wheelchair by ~12, death by ~20s). In-frame deletions maintain the reading frame → truncated, partially functional dystrophin → milder Becker MD (ambulatory into adulthood, normal lifespan possible). This is the molecular basis distinguishing DMD from BMD. Both are X-linked recessive. Muscle biopsy in DMD: absent dystrophin on immunostaining; in BMD: reduced/abnormal dystrophin.


## Buzzword Combination (Correct)

Frameshift deletion = premature stop = absent dystrophin = DMD (severe); In-frame deletion = truncated dystrophin = BMD (mild); same DMD gene

## Wrong Options

### 1: B) The in-frame deletion causes complete absence of dystrophin, which paradoxically results in a milder phenotype due to compensatory utrophin upregulation

Complete absence of dystrophin is the hallmark of Duchenne (frameshift mutation), not Becker. While utrophin upregulation is a real therapeutic target being studied for DMD, it does not explain the milder phenotype in this patient. His biopsy shows reduced but present dystrophin, consistent with an in-frame Becker mutation.

**Buzzword Combo:** Absent dystrophin = DMD (frameshift); reduced/present dystrophin = BMD (in-frame); utrophin upregulation = research target, not explanation for natural Becker phenotype

### 2: C) Becker MD results from a point mutation in the dystrophin gene, while Duchenne results from a deletion

Both DMD and Becker MD most commonly result from deletions in the dystrophin gene. The critical distinction is not the mutation TYPE (deletion vs point mutation) but the mutation's effect on the reading frame: out-of-frame deletion → DMD; in-frame deletion → BMD. Point mutations can also cause either phenotype depending on their effect on the reading frame.


**Buzzword Combo:** DMD vs BMD = frameshift vs in-frame (reading frame effect), NOT deletion vs point mutation


### 3: D) His milder phenotype is explained by X-inactivation protecting one copy of the dystrophin gene

X-inactivation occurs in females (46,XX),  one X chromosome is inactivated in each cell. This patient is male (46,XY) with only one X chromosome, so X-inactivation does not apply. Female carriers of DMD mutations may have mild symptoms due to skewed X-inactivation, but this mechanism is irrelevant for an affected male.


**Buzzword Combo:** X-inactivation = female phenomenon (two X chromosomes); does not apply to affected males with only one X chromosome
