## Buzzword Question

62-year-old man with a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease (baseline creatinine 2.1 mg/dL) presents for a routine follow-up. He was recently started on lisinopril for hypertension and diabetic nephropathy. Laboratory studies today reveal a serum potassium level of 6.2 mEq/L (up from 4.6 mEq/L two months ago) and a serum bicarbonate level of 20 mEq/L. An ECG shows peaked T waves. Which of the following alterations in renal collecting duct physiology is primarily responsible for his hyperkalemia?

## Buzzwords Used

Chronic kidney disease; diabetic nephropathy; lisinopril; serum potassium 6.2 mEq/L; peaked T waves

## Answer Options

A) Decreased expression and activity of apical Epithelial Sodium Channels (ENaC)
B) Inhibition of the basolateral Na+/K +ATPase pump in the proximal convoluted tubule
C) Impaired intracellular shift of potassium driven by insulin deficiency
D) Reduced glomerular filtration rate leading to decreased absolute delivery of water to the loop of Henle

## Correct Answer

A

## Explanation

ACE inhibitors like lisinopril suppress aldosterone production. Because aldosterone normally upregulates the expression and activity of apical ENaC channels and basolateral Na+/K+ATPases in the principal cells of the cortical collecting duct, its absence reduces the lumen-negative electrical potential required to secrete potassium into the urine.

## Buzzword Combination (Correct)

ACE inhibitor + Hypoaldosteronism + Reduced principal cell negative potential = Decreased ENaC/Potassium excretion

## Wrong Options

### B) Inhibition of the basolateral Na+/K +
 -ATPase pump in the proximal convoluted tubule

The primary site of aldosterone-regulated potassium excretion is the principal cells of the late distal tubule and cortical collecting duct, not the proximal convoluted tubule.

**Buzzword Combo:** Proximal tubule dysfunction + Bicarbonate wasting + Glucosuria = Fanconi syndrome

### C) Impaired intracellular shift of potassium driven by insulin deficiency

While insulin deficiency can cause hyperkalemia by reducing the cellular uptake of potassium via Na+/K +-ATPase in skeletal muscle, this represents a transcellular shift abnormality rather than a renal collecting duct physiologic mechanism.

**Buzzword Combo:** Diabetic ketoacidosis + Lack of insulin + Extracellular potassium shift = Transcellular hyperkalemia

### D) Reduced glomerular filtration rate leading to decreased absolute delivery of water to the loop of Henle

While a reduced GFR can limit absolute potassium filtration, hyperkalemia in patients on ACE inhibitors is specifically driven by a lack of distal tubule aldosterone action rather than simple changes in loop of Henle fluid delivery.

**Buzzword Combo:** Acute renal failure + Oliguria + Complete lack of solute delivery = Oliguric hyperkalemia
