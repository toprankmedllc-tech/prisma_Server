## Buzzword Question

A 79-year-old woman with advanced Alzheimer's disease is intubated in the ICU following aspiration pneumonia. She lacks decision-making capacity. She has a living will stating 'no mechanical ventilation if there is no reasonable chance of recovery.' Her healthcare proxy (her son) insists on continuing ventilation. Her daughter agrees with the living will. What is the most appropriate next step?


## Buzzwords Used

Alzheimer's; intubated; lacks capacity; living will; no ventilation if no recovery; son is proxy insists on continuing; daughter agrees with living will

## Answer Options

A) Follow the living will,  it reflects the patient's own prior autonomous wishes and takes precedence over the proxy's decision when directly applicable 
B) Follow the son's (proxy's) instructions because he has legal authority as healthcare proxy C) Follow the majority family decision (daughter agrees with living will, so 2-1 favors withdrawal) 
D) Continue ventilation until a court order is obtained to clarify the conflict

## Correct Answer

A

## Explanation

The hierarchy of advance directives and surrogate decision-making: (1) Patient's current expressed wishes (if capacity present), highest priority; (2) Advance directive / living will, represents the patient's own prior autonomous instructions when they had capacity; (3) Healthcare proxy/durable power of attorney for healthcare,  authorized to make decisions not specifically addressed in the advance directive; (4) Surrogate decision-making hierarchy (spouse, adult children, parents, siblings). A living will takes precedence over a proxy's decision when the living will directly addresses the clinical situation at hand. The proxy's role is to implement the patient's wishes, not substitute their own. The son, as proxy, must follow the living will's instructions.


## Buzzword Combination (Correct)

Hierarchy: patient wishes > advance directive/living will > proxy decision > surrogate hierarchy; proxy must implement advance directive, not override it

## Wrong Options

### 1: B) Follow the son's (proxy's) instructions because he has legal authority as healthcare proxy

A healthcare proxy has authority to make decisions NOT addressed in the advance directive, they are not authorized to override documented instructions. When a living will directly addresses the situation (no ventilation without chance of recovery), the proxy's role is to implement those wishes. The proxy's personal preferences are irrelevant when explicit instructions exist.

**Buzzword Combo:** Proxy authority applies to decisions not covered by advance directive; proxy cannot override a directly applicable living will

### 2: C) Follow the majority family decision (daughter agrees with living will, so 2-1 favors withdrawal)

Medical decisions are not made by family vote. The living will represents the patient's own autonomous decision, which supersedes any family consensus or disagreement. Family input matters in the absence of advance directives, not when explicit instructions exist.

**Buzzword Combo:** Family vote ≠ valid decision-making mechanism; patient's own advance directive supersedes family consensus

### 3: D) Continue ventilation until a court order is obtained to clarify the conflict

Court involvement is appropriate for genuine legal disputes or when the advance directive does not directly address the situation. Here, the living will directly addresses mechanical ventilation without chance of recovery. The ethics committee (not courts) is the appropriate resource for internal conflicts before legal escalation.

**Buzzword Combo:** Court order = last resort for legal disputes; ethics committee = first resource for internal conflicts; living will directly applicable here → follow it
