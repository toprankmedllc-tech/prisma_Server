## Buzzword Question

A 10-year-old girl develops irregular, rapid involuntary movements of the limbs and face, along with emotional lability and clumsiness, 6 weeks after a sore throat that was treated with a partial course of antibiotics. The most likely cause is:

## Buzzwords Used

Child; chorea; emotional lability; recent untreated streptococcal pharyngitis 6 weeks earlier

## Answer Options

A) Huntington disease
B) Sydenham chorea 
C) Wilson disease
D) Drug-induced tic disorder

## Correct Answer

B) Sydenham chorea 

## Explanation

Sydenham chorea is a major Jones criterion for acute rheumatic fever. Antibodies generated against group A streptococcal antigens cross-react with neurons of the basal ganglia (molecular mimicry), producing chorea, emotional lability, and motor clumsiness in children weeks to months after pharyngitis.

## Buzzword Combination (Correct)

Child + chorea + emotional lability + recent untreated strep pharyngitis = molecular mimicry → Sydenham chorea (acute rheumatic fever)

## Wrong Options

### Huntington disease

HD presents in midlife (typically 30s-50s), not childhood, and requires an autosomal-dominant family history; pediatric onset is rare and usually presents as juvenile HD with rigidity, not chorea.

**Buzzword Combo:** Midlife chorea + AD family history + CAG repeats + caudate atrophy = Huntington disease

###  Wilson disease

Wilson disease presents in adolescents/young adults with hepatic dysfunction and movement disorders (tremor, dystonia, parkinsonism), Kayser-Fleischer rings on slit-lamp exam, and low ceruloplasmin, not isolated post-infectious chorea.

**Buzzword Combo:** Young patient + tremor/dystonia + low ceruloplasmin + Kayser-Fleischer rings = Wilson disease

### Drug-induced tic disorder

Tics are stereotyped, repetitive movements that the patient can briefly suppress, often associated with stimulant medications. Chorea is irregular, non-stereotyped, and not voluntarily suppressible, and there is no medication exposure described.

**Buzzword Combo:** Repetitive stereotyped movements + temporary voluntary suppression ± stimulant exposure = tic disorder
